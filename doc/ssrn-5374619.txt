What Predicts Broadband Usage? A Comparison of Computer Vision and Rural
Urban Typology Machine Learning Models
Abstract
This study compares the predictive power of Google Street View (GSV) imagery against simple
geographic typologies for modeling broadband usage across 264 ZIP codes in eastern Nebraska.
We analyzed 7,600 GSV images using feature engineering and deep learning, evaluating their
performance against models based on Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes.
Our analysis revealed a large performance gap. Visual features from the built environment
explained almost no variance in broadband usage (R2~=0.025). In contrast, a location's RUCA
code, when analyzed with non-linear ensemble models, proved to be a strong predictor
(R2~=0.39). We conclude with a principle of efficiency: for visually homogeneous regions, the
most resource-efficient path to guiding broadband investment is to first unlock the rich, non-
linear information within existing geographic data.
1. Introduction
With tens of billions of dollars allocated to close the U.S. digital divide, the need for cost-
effective methods to identify and prioritize underserved communities has never been more
critical. The factors driving broadband adoption vary significantly with geography, demanding
place-based methods that can accurately guide investment from dense urban cores to sparse rural
landscapes. To develop effective policy, researchers must understand which data sources provide
the most reliable signals for these different environments.
Recent methodological advances in machine learning and street imagery offer promising tools to
reassess broadband access with reduced time and resources. In particular, the integration of
street-level imagery and machine learning may present a new opportunity to model and predict
infrastructure gaps with improved accuracy to more accurately direct financial resources. These
innovations shift the focus from retrospective analysis to predictive frameworks that could
inform more efficient and equitable infrastructure deployment.
This pilot study implements a method to study broadband inequality that is taken from adjacent
geographical work. This adjacent work leverages computer vision and statistical modeling to
attempt to model and predict local-level broadband needs. Central to this inquiry is the viability
of various demographic, socio-economic, and visual indicators in forecasting infrastructure
presence and accessibility. By evaluating which variables and analytic approaches serve as the
most robust predictors, this study aims to contribute to a data-informed framework for
identifying underserved communities and informing future broadband policy infrastructure
investments.
2. Literature Review
2.1 Introduction
Reliable broadband access remains uneven across the United States, with rural areas persistently
lagging suburban and urban counterparts despite decades of public investment and regulatory

reform (Ali, 2021; Strover, 2019). Early explanations centered on infrastructure scarcity, but
contemporary scholarship frames the "rural digital divide" as an interaction among geography,
market incentives, socio economic context, and policy design (Hollman et al., 2024a, 2024b).
This review captures major theoretical and methodological advances while incorporating recent
work on street level imagery and machine learning.
2.2 Policy and Funding Landscape
2.2.1 Federal Funding Mechanisms and Constraints
The Universal Service Fund (USF) and related high-cost programs have long been the principal
federal tools for equalizing rural and urban service costs (Ali, 2021). Yet analysts argue that the
USF's assessment base is shrinking even as its obligations grow, raising concerns about long run
sustainability (Mejia, 2024; Glass & Tardiff, 2021). Ongoing litigation over the program's
constitutionality further clouds the funding outlook (Class et al., 2024). Other infrastructure
funding mechanisms at state and federal levels abound. Broadband Equity, Access, and
Deployment (BEAD), Affordable Connectivity Program (ACP), USDA Community Connect
grants, and the Farm Bill, and ReConnect programs have all sought to expand broadband
infrastructure and increase broadband speeds in underserved and rural areas. Researchers have
found that these programs appear to have a positive effect on the availability of broadband in
rural areas, which has many economic implications (Deller et al., 2022; Whitacre et al., 2014).
Whitacre and Gallardo (2020) find that states with dedicated broadband offices and permissive
rules for cooperative or municipal networks achieve significantly higher fiber and 25/3 Mbps
standardized broadband speed coverage than states restricting such models. However, Kandilov
et al. (2017) noted that the positive impacts of broadband programs are often seen in counties
adjacent to metropolitan areas, including higher-speed internet; in non-adjacent areas, there is no
positive impact. Goldstein & Pender (2025) suggest that the USDA Community Connect
broadband program increased upload speed, but not download in rural areas. Furthermore,
Pender et al. (2023) showed Community Connect and ReConnect programs had significant
disparities in access among different racial and ethnic groups. The programs' approved service
areas had demographics which were "less educated, poorer, and older" compared not approved
service areas.
With the ACP ending on June 1, 2024, and a new Federal Communications Commission Chair
appointed, broadband policy may shift and form in a different direction (Benton Institute, 2024).
If broadband funding continues to dwindle, rural areas may be burdened with more regulation
costs as studies show that regulatory cost recovery can disproportionately burden sparsely
populated exchanges (Obermier & Hollman, 2020). Financial modeling indicates that stand alone
rural broadband cooperatives would need to charge upward of 130 percent of prevailing market
prices absent operating support (Schmit & Severson, 2021). States that ban or impede such
cooperative or municipal models thereby restrict potentially viable pathways to serve remote
areas (Whitacre & Gallardo, 2020). ACSE (2025) estimates that the private sector has spent $2.2
trillion with federal funding recently providing an additional $65 billion with the Infrastructure
Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA). This reinforces the need for granular cost modeling so that
federal and private funding dollars are directed to infrastructure efficiently and effectively.
2.2.2 Rural Disparities in Current Broadband Mapping Systems
Effective policymaking depends on accurate coverage data, yet the Federal Communications
Commission (FCC) historically collected information at census block granularity, a resolution
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=5374619
that systematically overestimates rural availability (Grubesic & Mack, 2015). Consumer reported
speed tests continue to expose mismatches between official maps and on the ground performance
(Hollman et al., 2024a; Ookla, 2021). Crowdsourced datasets must be interpreted with caution
because low-income households, which are often in remote areas, are underrepresented (Saxon &
Black, 2022). American Community Survey (ACS) Estimates report an adoption gap with only
87 percent of rural households having a broadband subscription compared to 91 percent of urban
households (Mejia, 2024) while ASCE (2025) says that 95 percent of consumers have access to a
fixed broadband technology. Recent research continues to highlight limitations in official
mapping systems and offer additional solutions. For example, Major et al. (2020) reverse
engineered Internet service provider (ISP) availability checkers and submitted more than 19
million address queries across nine states. Their dataset shows that FCC broadband maps
significantly overstate availability and speeds, particularly in rural and minority communities,
and demonstrates that automated address queries can produce more accurate coverage maps.
To create quality broadband maps, geographic information systems (GIS) are employed. These
systems take different data and attempt to represent this data, spatially. To do so, spatial areas
must be defined as rural or urban. However, there are many nuances in rural areas. In sparsely
rural areas, there may be a population of less than 250 people in 100 square miles. While in other
types of rural areas, there are 50,000 people within 100 square miles. Treating "rural" as a single
category masks substantial variation in settlement pattern, terrain, and socio-economics (Strover,
2019; Hollman et al. 2024a). To reclassify rural areas in multiple zones, Hossain et al. (2024)
combined demographic layers with network data to designate priority zones for broadband
expansion. Kostelnick et al. (2024) fused LiDAR1, crop yield and parcel datasets to locate both
unserved high value cropland and nearby "vertical assets" (e.g., silos, water towers) suitable for
fixed wireless antennas. In the rural areas, broadband is needed not only for consumer-based
internet, but also for precision agriculture (Kim & Paudel, 2025).
2.3 Emerging Methodological Innovations
2.3.1 Computer Vision and Street Imagery for Infrastructure Assessment
A critical barrier to rural deployment is the lack of detailed broadband infrastructure inventories.
Although service providers self-report their current infrastructure for FCC Broadband Data
Collection, the provider can report on future infrastructure that is not currently deployed.
Computer vision (CV) methods may offer a remedy. Krell et al. (2023) leveraged convolutional
neural networks (CNNs) and satellite imagery to detect cell tower footprints with more than 80
percent precision. This automated the mapping of cell tower structures and the subsequent
broadband cell coverage. Structural civil engineering research models the value of CV for
identifying poles, cables and other linear assets, which are otherwise prone to human inventory
errors (Koch et al., 2015; Chow et al., 2020). Amini (2024) also explored CV and deep learning
to engineer and optimize fixed wireless placement of equipment to provide broadband access in
mountainous terrain. Since both fixed and wireless broadband access require visible, physical
infrastructure, CV could present a feasible method for mapping broadband infrastructure.
Recent work extends CV to ground level imagery. In 2017, Gebru et al. collected 50 million
Google Street View images across 200 U.S. cities and used CNNs to identify the make and
model of 22 million automobiles. When aggregated, these detections allowed them to estimate

income, race, and voting patterns at precinct level granularity. Liu et al. (2017) introduced a
place centric multi-instance regression framework that treats each location as a "bag" of street
view photos and uses a deep CNN with an expectation maximization algorithm to predict safety
scores derived from crime records. Their method shows how variable length sets of images can
be mapped to continuous socioeconomic labels. Public health researchers have processed more
than 31 million street view images to detect built environment features such as crosswalks,
housing types, single lane roads and visible utility wires for 2,916 U.S. counties; these indicators
were then linked to obesity, physical inactivity and self-rated health outcomes (Nguyen et al.,
2021). Although street view imagery can offer rich detail for specific places, Egli et al. (2019)
points out that certain street views can be blurry making object identification less reliable. Time-
based scenic and signage items that are in constant change can also make analyses of specific
behavior phenomena less dependable. Fan et al. (2025) propose an indicator framework to assess
the completeness of the image to continue to eliminate bias when using street imagery datasets.
In this pilot dataset, Fan et al. (2025) found that only 62.4 percent of the buildings in London
were represented in street view imagery and only 12.4 percent of the building facade. They also
cautioned against using imagery for environmental elements.
2.3.2 Integrating Social Science Data with Quantitative Results
To understand the complex interactions of placement of broadband infrastructure and customer
needs, integrating broadband quantitative metrics, socio economic indicators, and behavioral
data could be the next step. Frameworks for multimodal machine learning (Baltrusaitis et al.,
2018) enable fusion of visual, textual, and numerical modalities. Saxon and Black (2022) merge
crowdsourced speed tests with income data, while Agarwal and Canfield (2024) embed the
Theory of Planned Behavior in an agent-based model that reveals price elastic tipping points for
broadband subscriptions in rural Missouri. The recent advances in CV on street view imagery
could provide additional modalities to produce a new method for examining broadband including
combining image derived indicators with broadband metrics, demographic, and behavioral
datasets to predict broadband use or to predict placement of new broadband infrastructure.
Attention based multiple instance models, such as the place centric approach of Liu et al. (2017),
demonstrate how unordered sets of images can be aggregated to a single label, an architecture
that is directly applicable to predicting broadband availability from multiple street view frames.
2.4 Exploring a New Method of Broadband Measurement
This recent scholarship points us towards the purpose of this paper. Rural broadband availability
and infrastructure lags behind urban counterparts despite the quantity of federal funding.
Although methods exist to measure rural broadband, these methods consume a lot of time and
monetary resources. Many typologies of rural also exist that are hard to distinguish in current
policy and research. CV and street imagery techniques offer an alternative method to predict
broadband availability in rural areas, which needs to be explored. This pilot study attempts to
explore the viability of various demographic, socio-economic, and visual indicators in
forecasting broadband infrastructure presence and accessibility. By evaluating which variables
and analytic approaches serve as the most robust predictors, this study aims to contribute to a
data-informed framework for identifying underserved communities and informing future
broadband policy infrastructure investments.
3. Data and Methods
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=5374619
Our study integrates multiple data sources to predict broadband usage across 264 ZIP Code
Tabulation Areas (ZCTAs) in Nebraska, an area selected to represent a mix of urban, suburban,
and rural environments. The methodology is defined by our input features, the target variable
used for model training, and our evaluation framework.
3.1 Input Features
3.1.1 Google Street View Imagery and Sampling
To create a representative visual dataset, we implemented a systematic sampling and collection
protocol using Google Street View (GSV) imagery. Our process began by identifying valid
building locations within our study area using the Google Buildings Footprint dataset.
From this geographic index, we developed a script to randomly sample an initial manifest of 500
structures per ZIP Code, constraining our selection to buildings between 100 and 1000 m2 to
exclude anomalous structures like small sheds or large industrial complexes. Using this manifest,
the script systematically queried the Google Street View Static API for each location, filtering all
requests to retrieve only imagery captured within the last decade. For each building with
available imagery, the script downloaded a complete set of four images corresponding to the
cardinal directions, proceeding through the manifest until a full sample was collected for the ZIP
code.
This method proved effective. We successfully collected a final dataset of 7,619 images,
achieving coverage for 261 of the 264 target ZIP codes (98.9%). The protocol's robustness
ensured that every sampled location with GSV availability is represented by a consistent four-
image set, forming the foundation for our visual feature extraction.
3.1.2 Rural-Urban Typology Data
We used Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes as a proxy for the relative level of
development. RUCA codes classify U.S. census tracts using measures of population density,
urbanization, and daily commuting patterns. This provides a standardized and widely used proxy
for the urban or rural character of a location. We used ZIP disaggregation RUCA codes provided
by the U.S. Department of Agriculture. (2020). Each ZIP code in our dataset was assigned its
corresponding primary and secondary RUCA codes to serve as the core geographic features.
However, the models all use only RUCA1, the primary codes, as we found RUCA2 codes to
added little to model efficiency.
3.1.3 Visual Feature Engineering
To translate the visual characteristics of the built environment into quantifiable data, we adopted
a hybrid approach that combined traditional computer vision techniques with pre-trained deep
learning models. This process, implemented using the Python library OpenCV, systematically
extracted a rich set of metrics from each image.
Our feature engineering began with established methods for capturing specific visual elements.
To quantify texture and local patterns in building materials and landscapes, we employed a suite
of key point detectors including SIFT, ORB, AKAZE, and BRISK. The geometric regularity of
larger structures was assessed using Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG), while Haar
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=5374619
Cascades were used to detect the presence of specific infrastructural objects. These traditional
methods provided a granular inventory of the physical components within each scene.
In addition to these hand-crafted features, we leveraged a pre-trained MobileNet convolutional
neural network to capture a more holistic representation of the scene. This deep learning model
converted each image into a dense feature vector, or embedding, which represents complex
visual patterns tied to the overall socioeconomic context that are otherwise difficult to define
manually. This process was supplemented by calculating standard color metrics, such as color
diversity histograms and average saturation, to quantify the material composition of the
environment.
After this comprehensive feature set was calculated for every image, the data was aggregated
first to the building level and then to the ZCTA level. By taking statistical summaries such as the
mean and variance of the feature values across all images in each area, we produced a final,
interpretable feature vector for each location to be used in our predictive models.
3.2 Target Variable: Broadband Usage Data
To serve as the ground truth for our predictive models, we utilized the "U.S. Broadband Usage
Percentages" dataset from Microsoft Research. This publicly available dataset provides estimates
of broadband usage and availability at the ZIP code level across the United States, derived from
Microsoft's service and application telemetry. For our study, the specific target variable was the
estimated percentage of the population within each ZIP code accessing the internet at or above
standard broadband speed tiers (25/3 Mbps). This continuous variable served as the label that our
machine learning models were trained to predict.
3.3 Modeling and Evaluation
To test the relative predictive power of visual versus geographic features, we developed and
evaluated several types of models. Our methodology included two classes of models: simple
regression models using only RUCA codes to establish a non-visual performance baseline; tree-
based ensemble models (including Extra Trees, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting) that
integrate both hand-crafted visual features and the RUCA codes.
Model performance was evaluated using a suite of metrics to ensure a comprehensive
assessment. The primary metric was the coefficient of determination (R2), used to quantify the
proportion of variance in broadband usage explained by each model. To measure the magnitude
of prediction errors, we also calculated the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and Mean
Absolute Error (MAE). To ensure the robustness of our results and prevent data leakage between
geographically proximate areas, we employed a group-based cross-validation strategy. This
approach keeps all images from a given ZIP code within the same fold, ensuring that the training
and testing sets remained geographically independent during model evaluation.
4. Results
Our analysis systematically evaluated multiple modeling architectures to predict broadband
usage, revealing a clear and consistent performance hierarchy. The findings demonstrate that
while visual features derived from Google Street View offer minimal predictive value, a simple
rural-urban typology contains a strong predictive signal, particularly when analyzed with non-
linear models.

A important finding emerges from analyzing the Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes

analysis revealed that the primary RUCA code (RUCA1) contained nearly all this predictive

4.3 The Limited Contribution of Visual Features
In contrast to the typology data, models relying solely on visual features performed poorly.
These models consistently failed to explain more than 3% of the variance in broadband usage,
achieving a negligible R2 of approximately 0.025. This result indicates that for this specific task,
region, and sample, the visual information from Google Street View provided little independent
predictive signal.
4.4 Deconstructing the Champion Model
The top-performing model, an Extra Trees Regressor with a final R2 of 0.41, was driven by its
ability to interpret the urban-rural typology data, not by a synergistic combination of features. A
detailed performance attribution analysis (Table 3) shows that the non-linear patterns within the
RUCA data alone accounted for nearly 60% of the model's total predictive power. The entire set
of hand-crafted visual features provided only a marginal 0.02 boost to the final R2 score, while
interaction effects between the feature sets were negligible.

5. Discussion & Conclusion
This study's findings offer a clear and compelling insight into modeling strategy for digital divide
research. The primary driver of predictive success in our models was not the sophisticated fusion
of multiple data modalities, but rather the application of an appropriate non-linear algorithm to a
simple, yet powerful, geographic typology. The Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) data
contained the foundational predictive signal, but its full potential-explaining nearly 40% of the
variance in broadband usage-was only realized when processed by an ensemble model like the
Extra Trees Regressor. The algorithm's ability to capture the complex, non-linear relationships
and threshold effects within the typology data alone accounted for most of the model's
performance.
Inefficacy of Street-Level Visual Data
We found minimal predictive power of the Google Street View imagery for a relatively small but
geographically diverse dataset of 7,600 images and the application of both hand-crafted feature
engineering and deep learning, the visual data in isolation had almost no value (R2~=0.025).
When added to the top-performing model, these features provided only a marginal, additive
boost of 0.02 to the final R2 score, with negligible interaction effects. This compels the question:
why did this intuitively rich data source fail? We speculate that the reasons are rooted in the
specific geographic and social context of our study area.
The core assumption that visual cues of the built environment are strong proxies for
socioeconomic status and infrastructure quality appears to break down in the geographical region
of Eastern Nebraska. The primary reason is likely visual homogeneity, particularly in residential
architecture. Unlike dense, historically layered urban centers where housing facades and square
footage can vary dramatically with income, the visual character of homes in Nebraska's
suburban, exurban, and rural areas is often quite similar. A modern, well-maintained home in a
rural ZIP code with limited broadband options may be visually indistinguishable from a nearly

identical home in a suburban development with ubiquitous fiber access. The visual cues of
affluence or upkeep do not map cleanly onto the urban-rural infrastructure gradient that defines
broadband availability. Consequently, the model could not learn a consistent relationship
between the appearance of a building and the quality of its digital connection. The success of
similar methods in other contexts, such as estimating demographics from automobiles in diverse
cities (Gebru et al., 2017), relies on visual indicators that are strongly correlated with the target
variable. In our case, the link is weaker. Furthermore, while our hand-crafted features were
designed to detect infrastructure like utility poles, the mere presence of a pole is not a
differentiator; it fails to distinguish between poles carrying legacy copper lines and those
carrying modern fiber optic cables. These critical differences are often invisible or too subtle for
a computer vision model to detect reliably from street-level imagery. Finally, while 7,600 images
is a substantial number, it averages to less than 30 images per ZIP code, a level of data scarcity
that may have been insufficient for a data-hungry CNN to learn meaningful patterns amidst the
visual noise.
Powerful Future Model
Although this learning model did not fully work as intended, the implications for future usage
need to be explored. Since the breakdown occurred within the inability to differentiate between
copper and fiber lines, perhaps the telecommunications providers could provide a differentiation
that would also enable better visual inventory inspection for civil engineers, similar to that done
for concrete structures/barriers and metal infrastructure. With small enhancements in the physical
environment, this model has the potential to predict future infrastructure that could enable
effective and efficient decision making by any policy maker, without needing to understand the
nuances of the technical, telecommunications environment.
Implications for Future Research
Even though the modeling did not fully predict as intended in this instance, this work offers a
clear, strategic path for future research. The primary lesson is that investigators should first seek
to maximize the predictive power of foundational, readily available datasets like geographic
typologies through the application of non-linear ensemble methods. This approach is
computationally and cost efficient and, as we have shown, can yield surprisingly strong results.
Only after exhausting the potential of such data should researchers consider incorporating more
complex and computationally expensive sources. However, our findings suggest a change in
focus may be warranted. Instead of street-level imagery, satellite or aerial imagery may prove
more effective. Satellite data captures a different class of features-such as land use patterns,
building density, vegetation cover, and the layout of transportation networks-that may correlate
more strongly with the rural-urban spectrum and, by extension, infrastructure deployment
patterns. Analyzing the broader spatial environment of a location, rather than the facade of its
buildings, is likely a more robust method for differentiating between areas in a visually
homogeneous region like Nebraska.

In conclusion, our study serves as a valuable case study in modeling strategy. It demonstrates
that the choice of model architecture and its ability to unlock non-linearities in existing data can
be more important than simply adding more data modalities. The value of visual data is highly
context-dependent, and its failure in this instance provides a crucial insight: the assumptions that
underpin successful machine learning applications in one environment cannot be transferred to
another without critical evaluation. By prioritizing nonlinear analysis of foundational data,
researchers can build strong, efficient, and highly predictive models to help bridge the digital
divide.
